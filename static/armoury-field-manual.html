1. Black-Scholes calculator

What it does

Prices a European call or put from five inputs: stock price, strike, time to expiry, interest rate and volatility (plus dividend yield).

Link to Black-Scholes

This is the model itself. Every other feature builds on it.

Visuals

2D shows option price against stock price. 3D shows option price against stock price and time to expiry. For colour, green means the market price is below the model price (the option looks cheap) and red means it is above (looks expensive). Amber means within 5% of the model.

The formula

d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r − q + σ²/2)·T] / (σ·√T) d2 = d1 − σ·√T Call = S·e^(−qT)·N(d1) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d2) Put = K·e^(−rT)·N(−d2) − S·e^(−qT)·N(−d1)

N() is the standard normal cumulative distribution: the probability a normally distributed variable lands below that value. N(d2) is roughly the chance the option finishes in the money; N(d1) also accounts for the size of the payoff when it does.

2. American options via a binomial tree

What it does

Prices options that can be exercised early (most US stock options), which Black-Scholes can't do.

Link to Black-Scholes

As you add more steps to the tree, it converges to the Black-Scholes price for European options. The difference between the two is the value of being able to exercise early.

Visuals

2D shows the branching price tree and how the tree converges as steps increase. 3D shows the early-exercise value against stock price and time. Colour highlights the nodes where exercising early is optimal (green).

The formula

u = e^(σ·√Δt) d = 1/u p = (e^((r−q)Δt) − d) / (u − d) Node value = max( exercise value, e^(−rΔt)·[p·Up + (1−p)·Down] )

The tree starts at today's price and splits into an up move and a down move at each step. Working backwards from expiry, at every node we compare holding the option with exercising it now and keep the larger. Where exercising wins, the node is flagged.

3. Implied volatility and the volatility smile

What it does

Works backwards from market prices to the volatility the market expects, then plots it across strikes and expiries.

Link to Black-Scholes

You get it by inverting Black-Scholes. The smile exists because real markets break the model's assumption of a single constant volatility.

Visuals

2D shows the smile (IV against strike). 3D shows the volatility surface (IV against strike and expiry). For colour, a cool-to-hot scale (blue = low IV, red = high IV) shows where options are relatively cheap or expensive. The verdict colour shows how much of the chain inverted cleanly.

The formula

Find σ such that BlackScholes(S, K, T, r, q, σ) = market price (solved by bisection, since price rises steadily with σ)

Out-of-the-money puts and calls are used because they carry the cleanest information. If IV is higher for low strikes than high strikes, the market is paying up for downside protection; that tilt is called skew.

4. Greeks and portfolio-level Greeks and risk

What it does

Measures how sensitive an option's price is to each input. Delta covers price, gamma how fast delta changes, theta time, vega volatility and rho interest rates. The portfolio view adds these up across all positions.

Link to Black-Scholes

Each Greek is a partial derivative of the Black-Scholes formula.

Visuals

2D shows each Greek against stock price, plus portfolio profit and loss against price. 3D shows a Greek against price and time (e.g. a gamma surface). For colour, green marks exposure within your limits, amber nearing a limit (over 70%) and red over it.

The formula

Delta = ∂V/∂S Gamma = ∂²V/∂S² Theta = ∂V/∂t Vega = ∂V/∂σ Rho = ∂V/∂r Portfolio Greek = Σ (contracts × 100 × position Greek)

Vega is quoted per one volatility point and theta per calendar day. The stress grid re-prices every position under price and volatility shocks, and the one-day VaR re-prices the book after a 95%-confidence one-day move.

5. IV rank and IV percentile

What it does

Shows where today's IV sits compared with its range over the past year (0–100).

Link to Black-Scholes

It uses the implied volatilities from the inverted Black-Scholes model, tracked over time.

Visuals

2D shows volatility over the past year with today marked, plus a gauge from 0 to 100. 3D shows IV rank across several stocks and lookback windows. For colour, low (green) suggests options are cheap for buyers and high (red) suggests they're rich for sellers.

The formula

IV rank = (today − 1y low) / (1y high − 1y low) × 100 IV percentile = % of days in the past year with IV below today's

Rank cares about the extremes; percentile cares about how often. They can differ a lot after a single spike. Free historical IV isn't available for each stock, so the calculator uses 21-day realised volatility as a stand-in unless you use a volatility index such as ^VIX, or type in your option's current IV.

6. Put-call parity checker

What it does

Checks that matching call and put prices are consistent: C − P = S − K·e^(−rT).

Link to Black-Scholes

Black-Scholes prices always satisfy parity, so when market prices don't, it points to bad data, dividends, borrowing costs or (rarely) an arbitrage opportunity.

Visuals

2D shows the parity gap at each strike. 3D shows the gap across strike and expiry. For colour, green means within tolerance and red means a mismatch.

The formula

Gap = (C − P) − (S·e^(−qT) − K·e^(−rT))

You set the tolerance in dollars; a sensible starting point is the bid-ask spread. Amber means the gap is up to three times the tolerance.

7. Volatility-adjusted position sizing

What it does

Suggests how many contracts to trade based on maximum acceptable loss or volatility exposure, rather than a fixed number.

Link to Black-Scholes

It uses vega and delta from the model, plus the expected move based on IV, to estimate risk per contract.

Visuals

2D shows position size against IV. 3D shows position size against IV and account risk %. For colour, green means within the risk budget and red means oversized.

The formula

Expected move = S · IV · √(holding days / 365) Risk per contract = loss when the model re-prices after a 1σ adverse move and a 20% IV shift against you Max contracts = floor( account × risk % / risk per contract )

Higher IV means a bigger expected move, so the same budget buys fewer contracts. That is the point: size shrinks when volatility rises.

8. Kalman filter

What it does

Separates the underlying trend from day-to-day noise in up to three tickers, and tracks a hedge ratio that is allowed to change over time.

Link to Black-Scholes

It sits beside Black-Scholes rather than inside it. Black-Scholes assumes constant volatility and drift; the Kalman filter lets both the trend and the relationship between tickers move.

Visuals

2D shows prices with the filtered trend, the hedge ratios over time and the spread z-score. 3D traces the hedge ratios through time, coloured by z-score. Green means the spread is normal, amber stretching (beyond 1) and red stretched (beyond 2).

The formula

Hedge ratio: Ticker 1 = β₂·Ticker 2 + β₃·Ticker 3 + intercept, where each β drifts a little every day Spread z-score = (Ticker 1 − fitted value) / forecast standard deviation Trend: local linear trend on log prices (level + slope)

At every new price the filter blends its prediction with the new data, trusting each according to its uncertainty. The adaptivity setting controls how quickly the ratios may drift.

9. Modern Portfolio Theory, Black-Litterman and risk parity

What it does

Finds long-only mixes of your tickers that balance return against risk, and reports a set of risk measures for each.

Link to Black-Scholes

Black-Scholes assumes returns are log-normal with a volatility. MPT uses the same two ingredients, expected return and volatility, but across several assets at once.

Visuals

2D shows the risk-return cloud of random mixes with the optimised portfolios marked, and growth over the period. 3D adds the Sharpe ratio as a third axis. Table cells go green, amber or red on common thresholds.

The formula

Sharpe = (return − risk-free) / volatility Sortino = (return − risk-free) / downside deviation Max drawdown = largest fall from a previous peak Calmar = return / |max drawdown| Treynor = (return − risk-free) / beta to the benchmark VaR 95% = the one-day loss exceeded on 5% of days CVaR 95% = the average loss on those worst 5% of days

Black-Litterman starts from equal weights as a neutral prior and nudges expected returns toward your views by the confidence you give them. Risk parity sizes each holding so it contributes the same amount of risk. Both can be switched on independently. Everything is in-sample, so it describes the past.

10. Strategy Builder

What it does

Combines the results you saved into a textbook structure, a list of risk checks and a conviction score from 0 to 100.

Link to Black-Scholes

It reads the model outputs, such as the Black-Scholes verdict and IV rank, and matches them with the structures that classic options theory pairs with them.

Visuals

The score is green at 70 and above, amber from 45 and red below that.

The formula

Score = 100 × (0.6 × average result quality + 0.4 × coverage), less 15 if results disagree on direction Quality: good = 1, caution = 0.5, poor = 0 Coverage = saved results ÷ 5, capped at 1

The score does not predict outcomes. It measures whether your saved analyses are sound and agree with each other. Saved plans feed the COT Report comparison.

11. COT Report

What it does

Searches the CFTC's Traders in Financial Futures report: weekly positions of dealers, asset managers, leveraged funds and others across financial futures.

Link to Black-Scholes

Separate from Black-Scholes. It adds context about who holds the other side of the market.

Visuals

Asset managers (institutions) and leveraged funds (hedge funds) are highlighted with their net position, share of open interest, four-week change and percentile over three years. The comparison tool lines this up with a saved Backtester strategy or Strategy Builder plan.

The formula

Net position = long − short Percentile = share of the last 156 weeks with a lower net position than today

The report covers financial futures such as stock indices, bond futures, currencies and bitcoin. It does not include gold or oil. Positions are as of Tuesday and published the following Friday, so the data is always a few days old. Data source: US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (public data).
