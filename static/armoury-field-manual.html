1. Black-Scholes calculator

What it does

Prices a European call or put from five inputs: stock price, strike, time to expiry, interest rate and volatility (plus dividend yield).

Visuals

2D shows option price against stock price. 3D shows option price against stock price and time to expiry. For colour, green means the market price is below the model price (the option looks cheap) and red means it is above (looks expensive). Amber means within 5% of the model.

The formula

d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r − q + σ²/2)·T] / (σ·√T) d2 = d1 − σ·√T Call = S·e^(−qT)·N(d1) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d2) Put = K·e^(−rT)·N(−d2) − S·e^(−qT)·N(−d1)

Key to the formula

S
Stock price: what one share costs today.
K
Strike price: the fixed price the option lets you buy (call) or sell (put) at.
T
Time to expiry in years. The calculator turns your days into years: days ÷ 365.
r
Interest rate as a decimal (4.5% = 0.045).
q
Dividend yield as a decimal (2% = 0.02).
σ (sigma)
Volatility as a decimal (25% = 0.25): how much the price tends to swing.
d1, d2
Two helper numbers worked out first, then fed into N().
ln( )
Natural logarithm. ln(S/K) measures how far the stock is above (positive) or below (negative) the strike.
e
Euler’s number, about 2.718. e^(−rT) shrinks a future amount to what it is worth today.
e^(−qT)
Takes off the dividends you would miss by holding the option instead of the share.
N( )
Normal distribution: turns a number into a probability between 0 and 1.
√
Square root.
σ²
Volatility squared (multiplied by itself).
·
Multiply.
/
Divide.
−d1, −d2
The same helper numbers with the sign flipped, used for puts.
Call / Put
The model price of each type of option.

N() is the standard normal cumulative distribution: the probability a normally distributed variable lands below that value. N(d2) is roughly the chance the option finishes in the money; N(d1) also accounts for the size of the payoff when it does.

In plain English: start here

An option is a contract that gives you the right, but not the duty, to buy or sell a share at a fixed price before a set date. You pay a small amount up front for that right. This calculator works out what that right should be worth. Here is what every box means.

Stock price
What one share of the company costs right now. If Walmart is $100, type 100. You can find it on any finance website or in your broker app.
Strike price
The fixed price written into the option. A call gives the right to buy at this price; a put gives the right to sell at it. A $105 call on a $100 share only pays off if the share climbs above $105.
Days to expiry
How many days until the option runs out. After that day it is worthless unless it is “in the money” (worth using). More days means more time for the price to move, so the option costs more. Historically, shares in general have grown 7–10% a year on average, with plenty of ups and downs along the way. Over 90 days, that works out at only about 2% on average.
Interest rate
What you could earn a year on cash kept somewhere safe, like a government savings bond. Use roughly 4–4.5% for US dollars, about 2% for euros and about 4% for pounds. It matters a little: higher rates make calls slightly dearer and puts slightly cheaper.
Dividend yield %
The cash a company pays its shareholders each year, as a percentage of the share price. A $100 share paying $1 a year has a 1% yield. Option holders don’t receive dividends, so a high yield makes calls cheaper and puts dearer. Type 0 if the company pays none.
Volatility %
How wildly the share price tends to swing in a year. 20% means a typical year sees the price move up or down by about a fifth. Steady companies sit around 15–25%; exciting or troubled ones 40% and up. Higher volatility makes every option more expensive, because a big move becomes more likely. This is the input that changes the price the most.
Type
Call: gains value when the price goes up (the right to buy). Put: gains value when the price goes down, which is also how people protect shares they already own (the right to sell).
Market price
What the option actually costs right now, shown in your broker app (use the midpoint between the bid and the ask). It’s optional: fill it in to see whether the option looks cheap or expensive against the model.
Model price
What the Black-Scholes formula says the option is worth, per share. Green means the market price is more than 5% below this (looks cheap), red means more than 5% above (looks expensive) and amber means within 5% (about fair). One contract usually covers 100 shares, so multiply by 100 for the real cost.
Delta
How much the option price moves when the share moves $1. Delta 0.40 means the option gains about 40 cents if the share rises $1. Calls are between 0 and 1; puts are between −1 and 0 (they gain when the share falls). It’s also a rough guide to the chance the option ends up worth using: 0.40 ≈ 40%.
Gamma
How fast delta itself changes when the share moves $1. A gamma of 0.03 means delta grows by about 0.03 for each $1 rise. High gamma means the option’s behaviour can change quickly; it’s highest when the share is close to the strike and expiry is near.
Theta / day
How much value the option loses each day just from time passing, with nothing else changing. −0.03 means it loses about 3 cents a day per share ($3 a day per contract). For the holder it is almost always negative: an option is like a melting ice cube.
Vega / 1 pt
How much the option price changes if volatility rises by 1 percentage point (say from 25% to 26%). Vega 0.15 means it gains about 15 cents. When the market gets nervous, volatility rises and options get dearer, even if the share price hasn’t moved.
Rho / 1 pt
How much the option price changes if interest rates rise by 1 percentage point (say from 4.5% to 5.5%). Usually tiny for short-dated options. Calls gain a little (positive) and puts lose a little (negative).

2. American options via a binomial tree

What it does

Prices options that can be exercised early (most US stock options), which Black-Scholes can't do.

Visuals

2D shows the branching price tree and how the tree converges as steps increase. 3D shows the early-exercise value against stock price and time. Colour highlights the nodes where exercising early is optimal (green).

The formula

u = e^(σ·√Δt) d = 1/u p = (e^((r−q)Δt) − d) / (u − d) Node value = max( exercise value, e^(−rΔt)·[p·Up + (1−p)·Down] )

Key to the formula

u
Up factor: how much the price multiplies by in one up step.
d
Down factor: how much it multiplies by in one down step (d = 1/u).
p
Risk-neutral probability of an up step; (1 − p) is the chance of a down step.
σ
Volatility as a decimal.
Δt (delta t)
Length of one step in years: time to expiry ÷ number of steps.
r, q
Interest rate and dividend yield as decimals.
e
Euler’s number, about 2.718. e^(−rΔt) brings a value one step in the future back to today.
√
Square root.
Up, Down
The option’s value at the two nodes one step ahead.
exercise value
What you would get by using the option right now: S − K for a call, K − S for a put (never below 0).
max( a, b )
Take whichever of the two is bigger.

The tree starts at today's price and splits into an up move and a down move at each step. Working backwards from expiry, at every node we compare holding the option with exercising it now and keep the larger. Where exercising wins, the node is flagged.

3. Implied volatility and the volatility smile

What it does

Works backwards from market prices to the volatility the market expects, then plots it across strikes and expiries.

Visuals

2D shows the smile (IV against strike). 3D shows the volatility surface (IV against strike and expiry). For colour, a cool-to-hot scale (blue = low IV, red = high IV) shows where options are relatively cheap or expensive. The verdict colour shows how much of the chain inverted cleanly.

The formula

Find σ such that BlackScholes(S, K, T, r, q, σ) = market price (solved by bisection, since price rises steadily with σ)

Key to the formula

σ
The volatility we are solving for: the implied volatility (IV).
BlackScholes( … )
The price from the Black-Scholes formula in section 1.
S, K, T
Stock price, strike price, time to expiry in years.
r, q
Interest rate and dividend yield as decimals.
market price
What the option actually trades for.
bisection
Guess a volatility, check the price, then halve the search range again and again until the price matches.

Out-of-the-money puts and calls are used because they carry the cleanest information. If IV is higher for low strikes than high strikes, the market is paying up for downside protection; that tilt is called skew.

4. Greeks and portfolio-level Greeks and risk

What it does

Measures how sensitive an option's price is to each input. Delta covers price, gamma how fast delta changes, theta time, vega volatility and rho interest rates. The portfolio view adds these up across all positions.

Visuals

2D shows each Greek against stock price, plus portfolio profit and loss against price. 3D shows a Greek against price and time (e.g. a gamma surface). For colour, green marks exposure within your limits, amber nearing a limit (over 70%) and red over it.

The formula

Delta = ∂V/∂S Gamma = ∂²V/∂S² Theta = ∂V/∂t Vega = ∂V/∂σ Rho = ∂V/∂r Portfolio Greek = Σ (contracts × 100 × position Greek)

Key to the formula

V
Value (price) of the option.
∂
“A small change in”. ∂V/∂S means “how much V moves when S moves a little”.
∂²V/∂S²
How fast that change is itself changing (the change of the change).
S
Stock price.
t
Time passing.
σ
Volatility.
r
Interest rate.
Σ (sigma)
Add up across every position.
× 100
One stock option contract usually covers 100 shares.

Vega is quoted per one volatility point and theta per calendar day. The stress grid re-prices every position under price and volatility shocks, and the one-day VaR re-prices the book after a 95%-confidence one-day move.

5. IV rank and IV percentile

What it does

Shows where today's IV sits compared with its range over the past year (0–100).

Visuals

2D shows volatility over the past year with today marked, plus a gauge from 0 to 100. 3D shows IV rank across several stocks and lookback windows. For colour, low (green) suggests options are cheap for buyers and high (red) suggests they're rich for sellers.

The formula

IV rank = (today − 1y low) / (1y high − 1y low) × 100 IV percentile = % of days in the past year with IV below today's

Key to the formula

today
Today’s implied (or stand-in) volatility.
1y low / 1y high
Lowest and highest volatility seen over the past year.
× 100
Turns the answer into a score from 0 to 100.
%
Share of days, out of 100.

Rank cares about the extremes; percentile cares about how often. They can differ a lot after a single spike. Free historical IV isn't available for each stock, so the calculator uses 21-day realised volatility as a stand-in unless you use a volatility index such as ^VIX, or type in your option's current IV.

6. Put-call parity checker

What it does

Checks that matching call and put prices are consistent: C − P = S − K·e^(−rT).

Visuals

2D shows the parity gap at each strike. 3D shows the gap across strike and expiry. For colour, green means within tolerance and red means a mismatch.

The formula

Gap = (C − P) − (S·e^(−qT) − K·e^(−rT))

Key to the formula

C
Call price.
P
Put price.
S
Stock price.
K
Strike price.
r, q
Interest rate and dividend yield as decimals.
T
Time to expiry in years.
e
Euler’s number, about 2.718. e^(−rT) and e^(−qT) bring future amounts back to today.
Gap
How far the real prices are from where theory says they should be. 0 means perfect.

You set the tolerance in dollars; a sensible starting point is the bid-ask spread. Amber means the gap is up to three times the tolerance.

7. Volatility-adjusted position sizing

What it does

Suggests how many contracts to trade based on maximum acceptable loss or volatility exposure, rather than a fixed number.

Visuals

2D shows position size against IV. 3D shows position size against IV and account risk %. For colour, green means within the risk budget and red means oversized.

The formula

Expected move = S · IV · √(holding days / 365) Risk per contract = loss when the model re-prices after a 1σ adverse move and a 20% IV shift against you Max contracts = floor( account × risk % / risk per contract )

Key to the formula

S
Stock price.
IV
Implied volatility as a decimal.
√
Square root.
holding days / 365
How long you plan to hold, in years.
1σ (one sigma)
A typical-sized move: about the expected move above.
account
Total money in your trading account.
risk %
Most you are willing to lose on this trade, as a share of the account.
floor( )
Round down to a whole number of contracts.

Higher IV means a bigger expected move, so the same budget buys fewer contracts. That is the point: size shrinks when volatility rises.

8. Kalman filter

What it does

Separates the underlying trend from day-to-day noise in up to three tickers, and tracks a hedge ratio that is allowed to change over time.

Visuals

2D shows prices with the filtered trend, the hedge ratios over time and the spread z-score. 3D traces the hedge ratios through time, coloured by z-score. Green means the spread is normal, amber stretching (beyond 1) and red stretched (beyond 2).

The formula

Hedge ratio: Ticker 1 = β₂·Ticker 2 + β₃·Ticker 3 + intercept, where each β drifts a little every day Spread z-score = (Ticker 1 − fitted value) / forecast standard deviation Trend: local linear trend on log prices (level + slope)

Key to the formula

Ticker 1, 2, 3
The prices of the stocks you entered.
β₂, β₃ (beta)
Hedge ratios: how many units of Ticker 2 and 3 move together with one unit of Ticker 1.
intercept
A constant gap left over after the betas.
fitted value
What Ticker 1 “should” be, given Tickers 2 and 3 and the betas.
forecast standard deviation
How big the filter expects the miss to be on a normal day.
z-score
How many normal-sized misses away we are. Beyond 2 is unusual.
log prices
Prices on a log scale, so a 10% move looks the same size at $10 or $1,000.
level, slope
The smoothed price and its direction (going up, down or flat).

At every new price the filter blends its prediction with the new data, trusting each according to its uncertainty. The adaptivity setting controls how quickly the ratios may drift.

9. Modern Portfolio Theory, Black-Litterman and risk parity

What it does

Finds long-only mixes of your tickers that balance return against risk, and reports a set of risk measures for each.

Visuals

2D shows the risk-return cloud of random mixes with the optimised portfolios marked, and growth over the period. 3D adds the Sharpe ratio as a third axis. Table cells go green, amber or red on common thresholds.

The formula

Sharpe = (return − risk-free) / volatility Sortino = (return − risk-free) / downside deviation Max drawdown = largest fall from a previous peak Calmar = return / |max drawdown| Treynor = (return − risk-free) / beta to the benchmark VaR 95% = the one-day loss exceeded on 5% of days CVaR 95% = the average loss on those worst 5% of days

Key to the formula

return
Average yearly gain of the portfolio.
risk-free
What you could earn with no risk (for example, a government bill).
volatility
How much returns swing up and down each year.
downside deviation
Volatility counting only the bad days.
beta
How much the portfolio moves when the benchmark moves 1%.
| x |
Ignore the minus sign (absolute value).
VaR 95%
Value at Risk: a loss exceeded on only 1 day in 20.
CVaR 95%
Conditional VaR: how bad those worst 1-in-20 days are on average.

Black-Litterman starts from equal weights as a neutral prior and nudges expected returns toward your views by the confidence you give them. Risk parity sizes each holding so it contributes the same amount of risk. Both can be switched on independently. Everything is in-sample, so it describes the past.

10. Strategy Builder

What it does

Combines the results you saved into a textbook structure, a list of risk checks and a conviction score from 0 to 100.

Visuals

The score is green at 70 and above, amber from 45 and red below that.

The formula

Score = 100 × (0.6 × average result quality + 0.4 × coverage), less 15 if results disagree on direction Quality: good = 1, caution = 0.5, poor = 0 Coverage = saved results ÷ 5, capped at 1

Key to the formula

×
Multiply.
÷
Divide.
average result quality
Average of your saved results’ quality scores (good = 1, caution = 0.5, poor = 0).
coverage
How many of the five key analyses you have saved, as a share from 0 to 1.
less 15
Take 15 points off if your results point in different directions.

The score does not predict outcomes. It measures whether your saved analyses are sound and agree with each other. Saved plans feed the COT Report comparison.

11. COT Report

What it does

Searches the CFTC's Traders in Financial Futures report: weekly positions of dealers, asset managers, leveraged funds and others across financial futures.

Visuals

Asset managers (institutions) and leveraged funds (hedge funds) are highlighted with their net position, share of open interest, four-week change and percentile over three years. The comparison tool lines this up with a saved Backtester strategy or Strategy Builder plan.

The formula

Net position = long − short Percentile = share of the last 156 weeks with a lower net position than today

Key to the formula

long
Contracts held that gain if the price goes up.
short
Contracts held that gain if the price goes down.
Net position
Long minus short. Positive = net long, negative = net short.
156 weeks
Three years of weekly reports.

The report covers financial futures such as stock indices, bond futures, currencies and bitcoin. It does not include gold or oil. Positions are as of Tuesday and published the following Friday, so the data is always a few days old. Data source: US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (public data).
