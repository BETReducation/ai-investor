Macroeconomics explainer

What an interest rate does to a government's targets

A central bank has one main lever and governments have several goals. This page traces how a change in the policy rate moves each goal, and why the answer depends on what caused the problem.

The question this started from: when inflation is driven by petrol, do rate rises work as intended? The last section answers it.

The goals being pursued

Most governments judge their economic management against a handful of macroeconomic objectives. Interest rates, set by an independent central bank in the US and UK, touch every one of them, and not always in the same direction.

Price stability

Low, steady inflation

The US Fed and the Bank of England both target 2% a year.

Growth

Output rising sustainably

Real GDP growing without overheating.

Employment

Low unemployment

The Fed has a formal "maximum employment" mandate alongside price stability.

External balance

Stable currency and trade

An exchange rate and current account that don't swing wildly.

Fiscal position

Manageable public debt

The government's own borrowing costs follow market rates.

How a rate change travels

Policy rateCentral bank moves its base rate
→
Market ratesBanks reprice loans, mortgages, savings
→
SpendingHouseholds and firms borrow and spend less (or more)
→
ObjectivesPrices, jobs, output, currency, debt costs

Borrowing cost

Dearer mortgages, cards and business loans cut demand. Savings pay more, which encourages holding cash over spending.

Asset prices

Higher rates lower the present value of future income, pulling down bond, equity and property prices and the wealth people feel they have.

Exchange rate

Higher rates attract foreign capital, lifting the currency. Imports get cheaper, exports less competitive.

Expectations

A credible bank that acts persuades firms and workers that inflation will stay low, so they ask for smaller price and wage rises.

The effects arrive with a lag. Estimates for the main impact on inflation typically run from one to two years.

What is driving inflation

Does it work when petrol is the cause?

Partly, and at a higher price. A rate rise cannot make crude oil cheaper. Global supply and demand set that price, and a single central bank's rate barely registers. What it can do is shrink everyone's other spending until the overall price index stops rising.

What is wrongSpending outruns the economy's capacity to supply.A key input costs more. Capacity itself has shrunk.
What a hike does to the causeRemoves it directly by cooling demand.Leaves it untouched. Only demand elsewhere is squeezed.

Where it does helpAcross the board.Stopping second-round effects: wage claims and price-setting that bake the shock in. A firmer currency also trims the local-currency cost of imported oil.

This is why economists debate "looking through" supply shocks. Once the first price jump has passed, headline inflation falls on its own as the base effect drops out. The risk is expectations: if people start assuming higher inflation is permanent, the temporary shock becomes a lasting one, and that is the part rates can influence.

Two episodes from the record

1973–1982, United States

Oil shocks and Volcker

Two oil shocks lifted inflation into double digits. Under Chair Paul Volcker the Fed pushed its policy rate to around 20% in 1981. Inflation fell sharply, but unemployment rose above 10% in 1982, the deepest US recession of the postwar era to that point.

2022–2023, United States

Post-pandemic and energy spike

Energy prices jumped after Russia invaded Ukraine, on top of strong post-lockdown demand. The Fed raised its target range from 0–0.25% in March 2022 to 5.25–5.50% by July 2023. US CPI inflation peaked near 9% in mid-2022 and then fell, while unemployment stayed historically low.

The two outcomes differ partly because the 2022 shock mixed demand and supply causes, and partly because expectations were better anchored by then. Historians and economists still argue about how much credit the rate rises deserve compared with easing supply chains and falling energy prices.
