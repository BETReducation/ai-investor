Trading Strategies: The Basics

A strategy is a set of rules decided in advance: when to get in, when to get out and how much to put at risk. This page covers the main families, shows two of them running on simulated markets, and explains why no single strategy works in every kind of market.

Educational and historical only. The price paths are simulated and nothing here is advice or a recommendation.

The idea in one minute

A strategy replaces in-the-moment decisions with written rules. That makes it testable: you can ask what the rules would have done on past prices, and see their good periods and bad ones before any money is at risk.

1. A strategy is a set of rules

Four parts: an entry rule, an exit rule, a size rule and sometimes a filter that says when not to trade at all. If any part is left to judgement on the day, it is not yet a strategy.

2. Two opposite ideas

Trend following buys what is rising and sells when the rise stops. Mean reversion buys what has fallen a long way from its average and sells when it returns. They are close to opposites, and each does well where the other does badly.

3. No strategy wins everywhere

Trend rules tend to do well when prices move in long runs and badly in choppy ranges. Mean reversion tends to do the reverse. A strategy's record depends on the kind of market it happened to face.

4. Judging a strategy

Look at more than the return: the worst fall along the way, how it compares with simply holding, how many trades it took and what costs they carried. A great backtest that came from trying many settings may be luck.
