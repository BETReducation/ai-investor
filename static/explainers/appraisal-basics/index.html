Appraising a Firm: The Basics

Four ideas cover most of the starting point: how much of its sales a firm keeps as profit, whether it can pay its short-term bills, what the market charges for each pound of earnings, and whether a new project is worth more than it costs. Each has a small calculator to play with.

Educational and historical only. The figures are fictional and nothing here is advice or a recommendation.

The idea in one minute

Looking at a firm is like a health check: a few simple readings, each answering one question. No reading decides anything alone, but together they show where to look closer. The examples use Harbour & Vale plc, a fictional distributor with revenue of £800m, operating profit of £100m, current assets of £250m, current liabilities of £150m, profit after tax of £64m and 200m shares priced at £4.80.

1. Profit margin

Operating profit ÷ revenue

Out of every £1 of sales, how much is left after the costs of running the business?

Harbour & Vale: 100 ÷ 800 = 12.5%, so about 12.5p of every £1.

Watch for: margins differ hugely by sector, so compare like with like and look at the trend.

2. Current ratio

Current assets ÷ current liabilities

Does the firm have enough short-term assets to cover what it owes within a year?

Harbour & Vale: 250 ÷ 150 = 1.67x, about £1.67 of short-term assets for each £1 due soon.

Watch for: textbooks often quote 1.5 to 2.0, but cash-trading firms such as supermarkets can sit lower, and a very high figure may mean idle cash.

3. Price/earnings (P/E)

Share price ÷ earnings per share

How many years of the firm's current earnings the share price adds up to.

Harbour & Vale: EPS = 64 ÷ 200 = 32p, so £4.80 ÷ 32p = 15x.

Watch for: a high P/E means the price already assumes strong growth, a low one that little is expected.

4. Net present value (NPV)

Σ cash flow ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ − outlay

Money later is worth less than money now, so future cash is shrunk to today's value using a discount rate, then the cost is taken off. Positive means the project beats the rate demanded.

Watch for: the answer is only as good as the forecast and the rate.
