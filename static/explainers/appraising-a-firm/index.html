Appraising a Firm: Ratios, Returns and NPV

No single number tells you whether a firm is sound. This page builds the full picture in layers: profit, liquidity, shareholder ratios, cash, and the methods used to judge new projects (NPV and average annual rate of return), then shows how they are read together.

Educational and historical only. The firms are fictional and the ranges are textbook rules of thumb. Nothing here is advice or a recommendation.

The idea in one minute

Appraising a firm means asking a few separate questions and then seeing whether the answers agree. Is it profitable? Can it pay its bills? Is it turning profit into cash? What do shareholders receive, and what does the share price already assume? And when it spends on a new project, does that project earn more than it costs, in today's money?

What the numbers cannot tell you

  • Why. A falling margin could be price cuts, rising costs or a one-off. The accounts show the effect, not the cause.
  • Peers. A ratio only means something against similar firms in the same sector and the firm's own history.
  • Quality of the figures. Profit depends on accounting choices. Cash flow is harder to flatter, so the two should be read side by side.
  • People and risks. Management track record, competition, regulation, customer concentration and the economy sit outside the ratios.
  • Price. A sound firm can still be a poor historical investment if the price paid already assumed a lot.

Real world

Two well-documented cases, described in outline. Figures are approximate and given for illustration; check primary sources before relying on any of them.

Carillion: profit that did not turn into cash

Carillion was one of the UK's largest construction and support-services groups. In July 2017 it issued a profit warning and wrote down the value of several contracts by roughly £845m. It entered compulsory liquidation in January 2018.

Reviews afterwards pointed to the same themes this page covers: heavy borrowing and a large pension deficit (gearing and cover), reliance on suppliers waiting a long time to be paid (payable days and liquidity), and reported profits that were not matched by cash coming in (cash conversion).

What it illustratesProfit ratios can look healthy while the cash and balance-sheet ratios are warning. That is why they are read together.

Cisco and the dot-com peak: a sound firm at a demanding price

In March 2000 Cisco briefly became the most valuable company in the world, valued at more than $500bn on a P/E ratio well over 100. By late 2002 its share price had fallen by more than 80% from that peak. Cisco stayed profitable throughout.

What it illustratesA high P/E means the price already assumes strong future growth. The business and the price are two separate questions, and the shareholder ratios address the second one.

Good habits

  • Compare with last year and with similar firms, never a single figure alone.
  • Check profit against cash flow.
  • Use the same discount rate across projects being compared.
  • Test NPV by changing the forecast, since forecasts are the weakest input.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a high current ratio always good: it can mean idle cash or unsold stock.
  • Comparing a supermarket's liquidity with a manufacturer's.
  • Using ARR alone: it ignores when the money arrives.
  • Quoting P/E for a firm with negative or one-off-inflated earnings.
  • Treating a rule of thumb as a pass mark.
