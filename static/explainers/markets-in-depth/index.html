Understanding Markets: Structure, Liquidity and Cycles

How prices are formed, how orders are matched, who takes part, what they trade and why markets move through booms and busts. Four labs let you push the mechanics around and see what happens.

Educational and historical only. Figures are rounded and illustrative, and nothing here is advice or a recommendation.

The idea in one minute

A market turns millions of separate decisions into one number, the price. To read it well you need three things: the mechanics (how orders meet), the cast (who is trading and why) and the mood (how sentiment pushes prices beyond what facts alone would justify).

1. How markets work

Price discovery, liquidity and the cost of trading.

Price discovery

Every buy and sell order carries information about what someone thinks an asset is worth. The market price is the running summary. New information, such as results or interest-rate news, shifts demand or supply and the price adjusts, often within seconds.

Liquidity

How easily you can trade a meaningful amount without moving the price. Deep markets have many orders close to the current price. Thin markets have few, so one order can move the price a lot and spreads are wide.

Watch for: liquidity is plentiful when conditions are calm and can vanish in stress.

Spread and slippage

The spread is the gap between the best bid and best ask. Slippage is the extra cost when an order is large enough to work through several price levels. Both are real costs that never appear on a quote screen as a fee.

Order types

A market order trades now at the best available prices, certain to fill but not of its price. A limit order sets the price and waits, certain of its price but not of filling. A stop order becomes a market order once the price reaches a trigger level.

Supply and demand shocks

A surge in buyers or a drop in sellers lifts price. Price sensitivity matters too: when buyers have few alternatives, the same shock moves price further. Commodities such as oil show this clearly because supply cannot change quickly.

How efficient is the price?

The efficient-market idea says prices already reflect available information, so they move mainly on news that has not yet arrived. It holds better for large, heavily followed assets than for small, neglected ones, and evidence of mispricing during bubbles and crashes keeps the debate alive.

2. Who is in the market, and what they trade

Different goals and time horizons all feed the same price.

Participants

Retail investors, pension funds, hedge funds, market makers, algorithmic traders, corporates and central banks. The Participants tab compares them.

Asset classes

Shares, bonds, currencies, commodities and crypto assets each have their own venues, hours and drivers. Shares trade on exchanges, most bonds trade between dealers, currencies trade around the clock on weekdays and crypto never closes.

Primary and secondary markets

In the primary market a firm issues new shares or bonds and receives the money. In the secondary market investors trade existing ones among themselves and the firm receives nothing. Almost all daily trading is secondary.

3. Cycles and sentiment

Why markets overshoot in both directions.

Bull and bear markets

By convention a bear market is a fall of 20% or more from a recent peak, and a bull market is a rise of 20% or more from a low. The thresholds are a convention, not a law.

Sentiment and herding

People tend to buy after prices have risen, when it feels safe, and sell after they have fallen, when it feels dangerous. When many do the same at once, prices overshoot their fundamentals on the way up and on the way down.

Leverage and forced selling

Borrowed money magnifies both gains and losses. When prices fall, leveraged holders must sell to meet margin calls, pushing prices lower and forcing more selling. This feedback loop explains why falls are often faster than rises.

Practise

Real world

Three well-documented episodes in outline. Figures are approximate; check primary sources before relying on them.

GameStop, January 2021: a short squeeze

Many hedge funds had sold GameStop shares short, borrowing and selling them in the hope of buying them back cheaper. Short interest was reported at more than 100% of the shares available to trade. When a wave of retail buyers pushed the price up, short sellers had to buy shares back to limit losses, which pushed it higher still. The price rose from about $20 at the start of the month to an intraday high near $483 on 28 January 2021, then fell sharply.

What it illustratesSupply and demand, leverage, forced buying and herding, all at once. The price had little to do with the firm's business during the spike.

The flash crash, 6 May 2010: liquidity disappears

In about 36 minutes the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell by roughly 9% and then recovered most of it. Some shares briefly traded at absurd prices, down to a cent. Investigations found that a very large sell order met buyers who stepped back, and that automated traders withdrew, so there were too few resting orders to absorb the selling.

What it illustratesA deep market can turn thin in minutes. Slippage in the Order book lab is the same effect in miniature.

The Covid crash, February–March 2020: speed of a sentiment shift

The S&P 500 fell about 34% in 33 days from its February peak, one of the fastest declines on record, as uncertainty about the pandemic spread. Central banks cut interest rates and began buying bonds, and the index had regained its high by August 2020.

What it illustratesFear can move prices faster than facts arrive, and central-bank actions change the price of money for every market.

Keywords

Good habits

  • Check liquidity before judging how easily an asset trades.
  • Treat the spread and slippage as real costs.
  • Ask who is on the other side of a trade, and why.
  • Separate what happened to the business from what happened to the price.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming you can always sell at the quoted price.
  • Reading a rising price as proof of rising value.
  • Treating the 20% bear-market line as a rule of nature.
  • Forgetting that leverage works in both directions.
