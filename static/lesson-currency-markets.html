Currency Markets & Capital Flows

Currencies move on interest rate differentials, trade balances, and where global capital is chasing the best risk-adjusted returns. A strengthening or weakening currency quietly reshapes the earnings of every multinational company you might own.

The Concept

Where the World's Capital Chases Return

A currency's exchange rate is, at its core, a relative price — how much of one currency it takes to buy another. That relative price moves as global capital flows toward whichever country offers the best risk-adjusted return at the time. The single biggest driver, tying directly back to the previous two lessons in this track, is the interest rate differential: when one country's central bank offers meaningfully higher rates than another's, capital tends to flow toward the higher-yielding currency to capture that difference, pushing its value up (all else equal).

A country's trade balance — exports minus imports — also matters. A country running a large trade surplus (exporting more than it imports) generates steady foreign demand for its currency, as foreign buyers need it to pay for those exports. A persistent trade deficit works in the opposite direction, creating a steady source of selling pressure on the currency over time.

Currency moves aren't just a forex-trading concern — they quietly reshape corporate earnings. A company that sells heavily overseas but reports in its home currency sees its foreign revenue translate into fewer home-currency dollars when its home currency strengthens (a stronger currency makes exports more expensive for foreign buyers, and shrinks reported overseas earnings on translation) — and the reverse when it weakens. This is why large multinational companies routinely flag currency effects explicitly in their earnings reports.

Watch For This

5 Things to Know About Currency Markets

  1. Interest rate differentials are a major driver, not the only one — trade balances, risk sentiment, and capital flows all interact simultaneously.
  2. Currencies affect multinational earnings both ways — a stronger home currency can hurt reported overseas earnings on translation, and vice versa.
  3. "Risk-on" and "risk-off" sentiment moves currencies too — some currencies (like the US dollar, Japanese yen, and Swiss franc) are often sought as havens during market stress, regardless of interest rate differentials at that moment.
  4. Central bank divergence (from the earlier lessons) is a direct currency driver — one country cutting while another holds or hikes widens the differential and often the currency move.
  5. Currency markets are enormous and trade nearly 24 hours a day — among the most liquid markets in the world, which shapes how quickly new information gets priced in.
Put It Into Practice

4 Things to Check When Reading Currency Moves

🏦 Compare the Two Central Banks

  • Check both countries' current rates and, importantly, expected future rate paths, not just today's level.

📦 Check the Trade Balance Trend

  • A persistent, widening deficit or surplus can be a slower-moving but real structural driver.

😰 Consider Risk Sentiment

  • In a broad market sell-off, safe-haven currency flows can temporarily override interest rate logic.

🌍 Check a Multinational's Currency Exposure

  • Before assuming a currency move is irrelevant to a stock, check how much of that company's revenue comes from overseas.
