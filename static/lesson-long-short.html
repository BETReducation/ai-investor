Going Short — Long, Short & Reversing

Going long profits when price rises; going short profits when it falls. The two are not mirror images: the risks, the costs and the exit logic all differ, and a strategy that flips between them needs rules for both.

The Concept

Four Events, Not Two

Most people first meet trading as "buy" and "sell". That works while you only ever go long, but it falls apart the moment a strategy can also bet on a fall, because "sell" then means two different things: closing a position you own, or opening a new bet against the price. Professionals separate the action (opening or closing) from the direction (long or short), which gives four events.

EventWhat happensOrder placedWins when
Long entryOpen a position you ownBuyPrice rises
Exit longClose the position you ownSell—
Short entryBorrow and sell, owing the shares backSellPrice falls
Exit short (cover)Buy the shares back and return themBuy—

Notice the order types cross over: a short entry is a sell order, and the cover that ends it is a buy order. That is why a bare "buy" or "sell" signal is ambiguous until you know whether it opens or closes a position.

How a Short Works

To short, you borrow shares from a lender, sell them at today's price and hold the cash. Later you buy the same number of shares back, return them, and keep or lose the difference. If you sold at $100 and bought back at $80, you made $20 a share. If you bought back at $120, you lost $20 a share. Return on a short is (entry price − exit price) ÷ entry price, the mirror of the long formula.

The results are exact opposites, but the limits are not. A long position can lose at most the full $10,000, because a price cannot go below zero. A short position has no ceiling: a price that triples loses twice the original stake.

Watch For This

5 Ways Shorts Differ From Longs

  1. The loss is uncapped — a long can go to zero, a short can keep losing as the price climbs, which is why a stop-loss matters more on the short side.
  2. Shorts carry a running cost — borrowing shares costs a fee, usually quoted as an annual rate, charged for every day the position is open. Heavily shorted stocks can be expensive or impossible to borrow.
  3. Time works against a short — long-run markets have tended to rise, so a short fights the broader drift as well as the borrow fee, and it needs to be right sooner.
  4. Squeezes are fast and violent — when many shorts are forced to buy back at once, the buying itself pushes the price higher, which forces more covering.
  5. The stop sits on the other side — a long's stop is below entry and its target above; a short's stop is above entry and its target below. A trailing stop follows the lowest price, not the highest.
Strategy Design

Stop-and-Reverse: One Position at a Time

Some systems are never flat. They hold a long until the evidence turns, then close it and open a short in the same move, and flip back again later. This is called stop-and-reverse. The rules are simple: only one position is open at a time, and an entry signal in the opposite direction closes the current position and opens the new one.

A related variant reverses on a stopped-out loss. If a long is stopped out because price fell through the stop, the strategy treats that fall as information and opens a short at the stop price instead of waiting on the sidelines.

Illustrative Example: Reversing on a Stop, Twice

A long is opened at $100 with a 5% stop, so it exits at $95. The strategy reverses into a short at $95, also with a 5% stop above entry at $99.75. Two different futures:

What Price Does NextLong LegShort LegCombinedLong-Only Instead
Keeps falling to $85−5.0%+10.5%+5.0%−5.0%
Bounces back to $99.75−5.0%−5.0%−9.8%−5.0%

When the trend continues, reversing turns a stopped-out loss into a net gain. When price whipsaws, the strategy is stopped out twice and the losses compound. Reversing is a bet on follow-through, and it costs more in choppy, sideways markets. Combined figures compound the two legs (0.95 × 1.105 and 0.95 × 0.95).

Price-Level Triggers

When Price Itself Is the Signal

Indicators are not the only way to define an entry. A price-level trigger acts when price crosses a chosen level: buy when it closes above, go short when it closes below. A common design is "hold a long above a support level, and when that level breaks, close the long and go short". The level can come from the chart (a prior low, a round number) or from a longer-term average.

Quick Check: 5 Questions

Answer all five, then hit "Check My Answers" to see how you did. Get one wrong? No problem — the explanation will show you exactly why.

0/5
Nice work — review any explanations below to lock it in.
1. Why is a bare "sell" signal ambiguous in a strategy that can go both long and short?
A sell order can end a long position or start a short one. Separating the action (open or close) from the direction (long or short) removes the ambiguity.
2. A short is opened at $50 and covered at $40. What is the gross return, ignoring costs?
Short return is measured against the entry price: (entry − exit) ÷ entry. The 25% figure divides by the exit price, which is the wrong base.
3. What is the key risk difference between a long position and a short position?
A price cannot fall below zero, so a long loses at most 100%. A price can keep rising, so a short's loss can exceed the original position.
4. Which cost applies to short positions but not to long positions held in cash?
Borrowing is what makes a short possible, and it is charged as an annual rate for as long as the position is open, so a slow-moving short can be eaten away by the carry.
5. In the lesson's stop-and-reverse example, what happens if price bounces back after the strategy reverses into a short?
Reversing assumes the move continues. In a whipsaw, the long loses 5% and the short then loses another 5%, for roughly −9.8% combined, worse than simply staying out.
