Quantitative Strategy Design

Trading ideas become durable when you turn them into rules a computer can test. Here's how professionals build and validate a systematic edge.

1
🔄Mean Reversion — Betting Prices Return to "Normal"

Mean reversion strategies bet that when a price strays far from its historical average, it will eventually snap back. Pairs trading and statistical arbitrage are built on this single idea, applied with strict rules instead of gut feel.

A backtest runs your strategy's rules against historical data to see how it would have performed. Done properly — with realistic costs and no lookahead bias — it's the difference between a hunch and evidence.

Options & Derivatives

Options let you define risk with precision most other instruments can't match — if you understand exactly what you're holding.

1
🎟️What Is an Option? Rights, Not Obligations

A call option gives you the right — not the obligation — to buy an asset at a set price before a set date; a put gives you the right to sell. That asymmetry, capped cost against uncapped potential, makes options a genuinely different tool from owning the stock outright.

2
🧮The Greeks — Delta, Gamma, Theta & Vega Explained

The Greeks measure how an option's price reacts to different forces: Delta to the underlying price, Gamma to the speed of that change, Theta to time decay, and Vega to volatility. Master these and you understand exactly what you're exposed to.

Open full lesson →
3
📊Implied Volatility & Volatility Skew

Implied volatility reflects what the market expects future price swings to look like — and it's rarely flat across strike prices. Understanding the resulting "skew" reveals where the market is pricing in the most fear or complacency.

4
💵Income Strategies — Covered Calls & Cash-Secured Puts

Selling a covered call against stock you own — or a cash-secured put on stock you'd be happy to buy — lets you collect a premium in exchange for capping your upside or committing to a purchase price. It's a way to get paid for a view you already hold.

5
🛡️Spreads & Hedging — Defining Your Risk on Both Sides

Combining two or more options into a spread — like a bull call spread or a protective collar — lets you cap both your cost and your risk, trading away some potential profit for a far more predictable outcome.

Macro & Cross-Asset Analysis

Zoom out. The biggest, longest-lasting moves in any single asset are often set in motion by forces far outside it.

1
🏦Central Bank Policy — Why Interest Rates Move Everything

When central banks raise or cut interest rates, they change the cost of money for the entire economy — reshaping everything from mortgage payments to how attractively priced stocks look compared to bonds. Few single forces move markets more.

Key Terminology

18 terms that professional trading desks and fund managers use every day — click any one to expand it, with a plain-English definition and a real example.

1
Basis Points (bps) — The Language of Precision ▾

A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point (0.01%). Professionals use bps instead of percentages because it removes ambiguity — "up 25bps" always means a quarter of one percent, no matter the starting number.

Example: A bond yield moving from 4.00% to 4.25% is described as "up 25 basis points" — precise and unambiguous, versus a vaguer "up a quarter percent."
2
Duration — A Bond's Sensitivity to Interest Rates ▾

Duration measures how much a bond's price will move for a given change in interest rates. A bond with a duration of 7 will fall roughly 7% in value if rates rise by 1% — essential for managing interest rate risk. Builds on: Central Bank Policy (Pro).

Example: A 30-year Treasury bond has much higher duration than a 2-year note, so the same 1-point rate move causes a far bigger price swing in the 30-year bond.
3
Leverage — Amplifying Gains and Losses Alike ▾

Leverage means controlling a larger position than your capital alone would allow, using borrowed money or derivatives. It multiplies your returns in both directions — a powerful tool that has also ended more accounts than almost any other single mistake.

Example: 5x leverage on a $10,000 account controls $50,000 of exposure — a 10% favorable move returns 50% on capital, but a 10% adverse move wipes out half the account just as fast.
4
Value at Risk (VaR) — Quantifying Your Worst-Case Day ▾

Value at Risk estimates the maximum expected loss over a given period at a given confidence level — for example, "95% confident we won't lose more than 3% tomorrow." It's the standard risk metric used across professional trading desks.

Example: A $10M portfolio with a 1-day 95% VaR of $300,000 is expected to lose no more than $300,000 on 95 out of 100 trading days — the other 5 days can be worse.
5
Liquidity Risk vs Market Risk — Two Different Ways to Lose ▾

Market risk is the chance an asset's price falls; liquidity risk is the chance you can't exit at a fair price even if you're right about the direction. Professionals plan for both — a great thesis is worthless if you can't act on it when it matters. Builds on: Liquidity (Intermediate).

Example: A thinly-traded small-cap stock can be a great pick (low market risk in the thesis) but still cost you dearly on the way out if there aren't enough buyers when you need to sell (high liquidity risk).
6
Z-Score — Measuring How Stretched a Price Is ▾

A z-score measures how many standard deviations a price sits from its historical average: (current price − mean) ÷ standard deviation. It's the core statistical tool behind mean-reversion strategies. Builds on: Mean Reversion (Pro).

Example: A stock with a 60-day mean of $100 and standard deviation of $5, now trading at $110, has a z-score of +2.0 — two standard deviations above its recent average, a level some mean-reversion rules flag as statistically stretched.
7
Factor — A Measurable Driver of Returns ▾

A factor is a specific, measurable characteristic historically associated with different returns across a group of securities — momentum, value, quality, and size are the most widely studied. Factor models rank a universe and hold the top slice systematically. Builds on: Momentum & Factor Models (Pro).

Example: A momentum factor ranks stocks by trailing 6-month return and holds the top decile — the same rule applied across hundreds of names, rather than a single stock-specific thesis.
8
Overfitting — When a Backtest Memorizes the Past ▾

Overfitting happens when a strategy's rules are tuned so precisely to historical data that they've essentially memorized the past rather than found a real, repeatable pattern. The more parameters adjusted to fit the data, the more likely the result is fitting noise. Builds on: Backtesting (Pro).

Example: A strategy tuned with 15 adjustable parameters to maximize its historical return looks spectacular in the backtest, but often collapses on new, unseen data — a classic overfitting red flag.
9
Market Regime — Conditions That Change the Rules ▾

A market regime is a broad market condition — low-volatility trending, range-bound/choppy, or high-volatility crisis — that shapes which strategies tend to work. A strategy that thrives in one regime can struggle badly in another. Builds on: Regime Detection (Pro).

Example: A momentum strategy can shine in a steady trending regime and get whipsawed in a choppy, range-bound one — the same rules, very different outcomes, purely because the regime changed.
10
Risk Parity — Sizing by Risk, Not Dollars ▾

Risk parity sizes each position so it contributes a roughly equal amount of risk to a portfolio, rather than an equal dollar amount — a more volatile asset gets a smaller dollar allocation for the same risk budget. Builds on: Position Sizing & Risk Budgeting (Pro).

Example: $100,000 in 6%-volatility bonds and $100,000 in 32%-volatility small-cap stock are equal in dollars but wildly unequal in risk — risk parity would size the small-cap position much smaller to balance the two.
11
Strike Price & Premium — The Core Language of Options ▾

The strike price is the fixed price at which an option can be exercised; the premium is the upfront cost paid to buy that right. Every options strategy is built from combinations of these two numbers across different contracts. Builds on: What Is an Option? (Pro).

Example: A $100-strike call bought for a $4 premium gives the buyer the right to buy the stock at $100, at a cost of $4 per share paid upfront regardless of outcome.
12
Delta — An Option's Directional Exposure ▾

Delta measures how much an option's price changes for a $1 move in the underlying stock — a delta of 0.60 means the option gains roughly $0.60 for every $1 the stock rises. It also roughly approximates the option's probability of expiring in the money. Builds on: The Greeks (Pro).

Example: A call option with a delta of 0.30 moving alongside a $5 stock rally would be expected to gain roughly $1.50 in value, all else equal.
13
Implied Volatility (IV) — The Market's Own Forecast ▾

Implied volatility is the volatility level that, plugged into an options pricing model, produces the option's actual current market price — a forward-looking estimate embedded in the option itself, distinct from historical (realized) volatility. Builds on: Implied Volatility & Skew (Pro).

Example: IV often spikes ahead of a company's earnings report as uncertainty rises, then drops sharply right after the results are known — a pattern called "volatility crush."
14
Assignment — When an Option Seller Must Deliver ▾

Assignment is what happens when an option is exercised against its seller — a call seller must deliver (sell) the shares at the strike; a put seller must buy them. Sellers who collect a premium take on this obligation in exchange for it. Builds on: Income Strategies (Pro).

Example: A covered call seller whose $110 strike is breached at expiration gets their shares "called away" at $110 — a form of assignment, capping their gain at that price regardless of how much higher the stock trades.
15
Vertical Spread — Defining Risk on Both Sides ▾

A vertical spread combines buying one option and selling another of the same type and expiration at a different strike, creating a position with a precisely defined maximum gain and maximum loss. Builds on: Spreads & Hedging (Pro).

Example: Buying a $100 call for $6 and selling a $110 call for $2 costs a net $4, caps the maximum loss at $4, and caps the maximum gain at $6 — a bull call spread trading unlimited upside for a bounded, cheaper position.
16
Yield Curve Inversion — The Bond Market's Warning Sign ▾

An inversion happens when short-term bond yields rise above long-term yields, flipping the normal upward-sloping curve. It typically reflects the market pricing in future rate cuts, often anticipating a coming economic slowdown. Builds on: The Yield Curve (Pro).

Example: The 2-year Treasury yielding more than the 10-year — a "2s/10s inversion" — is the most widely watched version of this signal, historically preceding most U.S. recessions by many months to over a year.
17
Interest Rate Differential — The Engine of Currency Moves ▾

The interest rate differential is the gap between two countries' interest rates. Capital tends to flow toward whichever currency offers the better rate, all else equal — the single biggest driver of currency direction. Builds on: Currency Markets & Capital Flows (Pro).

Example: If Country A's rate rises from 2% to 5.5% while Country B's stays at 1%, the widening differential has historically tended to attract capital toward Country A's currency, strengthening it.
18
Safe Haven — An Asset That Holds Up When Others Fall ▾

A safe-haven asset (gold, and certain currencies like the Swiss franc or US dollar) is one investors flock to during "risk-off" periods, often maintaining or strengthening a negative correlation to stocks exactly when that correlation matters most. Builds on: Commodities & Safe Havens (Pro).

Example: In a sudden equity sell-off, gold and a safe-haven currency often rise or strengthen even as stocks and other risk assets fall together — though this pattern is a strong historical tendency, not a guarantee in every crisis.
Worth knowing: this vocabulary is the floor, not the ceiling — professional desks layer dozens more specialized terms on top depending on asset class. Use this as your foundation for reading research and prospectuses with confidence.
