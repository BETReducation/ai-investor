Volume — How Much Conviction Is Behind the Move?

Volume — the bars beneath a chart — shows how many shares changed hands. A price move on high volume carries far more weight than the same move on a quiet, low-volume day.

The Concept

What Is Volume, Really?

Volume is simply the number of shares (or contracts, for other assets) that changed hands during a time period — a day, an hour, a minute, whatever the chart is set to. Most charting tools plot it as a bar underneath the price candles, one bar per period, so you can see both the price move and the activity behind it at a glance.

Volume matters because it tells you how much conviction is behind a price move. A stock jumping 5% on triple its normal volume means a lot of buyers and sellers actually showed up and agreed on that new price — that's a real, well-supported move. The same 5% jump on unusually thin volume means far fewer people participated, so the move is easier to reverse and less trustworthy as a signal.

  • High volume + big price move: strong conviction — a real, well-supported move worth paying attention to.
  • Low volume + big price move: weak conviction — few participants agreed on the new price, so it's more likely to fade or reverse.
  • Rising price on falling volume: a classic warning sign — the trend is running out of willing buyers even as price keeps drifting up.

📊 Same Price Move, Different Conviction

High-Conviction Rally
Price rises alongside rising volume bars — real buying interest, not just drift.
Low-Conviction Drift
Same price rise, but volume keeps shrinking — fewer buyers backing the move each day.

Same price chart shape, two very different stories. The first is a move you can have some confidence in; the second is the kind of quiet drift that often stalls or reverses the moment real volume shows up in the other direction.

That's a real, high-conviction move: an enormous number of investors were actively agreeing that AAPL's price needed to reset, not just a handful of traders nudging it around.

Watch For This

5 Things to Know About Volume

  1. Volume confirms a move; it rarely predicts one on its own — treat a volume spike as supporting evidence for a price move you're already looking at, not a standalone trading signal.
  2. Compare volume to its own recent average, not to a fixed number — 50 million shares is huge for a small-cap stock and nothing for a mega-cap like Apple. What matters is the multiple versus that stock's own normal volume.
  3. Breakouts on low volume are more likely to fail — a price punching through a trend line or resistance level needs volume behind it to be believable; a quiet breakout often snaps back.
  4. Volume tends to spike around news — earnings, product launches, economic data — because that's when the most people are reassessing what a stock is worth at once.
  5. A rising price on shrinking volume is a classic warning sign — it suggests fewer and fewer buyers are willing to keep pushing the price up, even as it keeps drifting higher.
Put It Into Practice

4 Things to Check When Reading Volume

📏 Compare to the Recent Average

  • Most charting tools can show a 20- or 50-day average volume line — use it as your baseline.
  • A day at 2-4x average volume is genuinely unusual and worth a closer look.

🔍 Check Volume at Breakouts

  • A trend line break on high volume is far more credible than one on a quiet day.
  • Low-volume breakouts fail more often than they hold.

📉 Watch for Divergence

  • Price making new highs while volume trends lower is a classic early warning sign.
  • It doesn't guarantee a reversal, but it's worth paying attention to.

📰 Expect Spikes Around News

  • Earnings reports, major announcements, and economic data releases routinely cause 3-5x normal volume.
  • Don't mistake a predictable news-driven spike for something unusual on its own.
Worth knowing: volume tells you how many people participated in a move, not which direction it will go next. High volume can accompany a genuine reversal just as easily as a continuation — pair it with the trend and price action around it rather than reading it alone.
Activity

Try It Yourself: Rate the Conviction

Enter a day's price change, its volume, and the stock's normal average volume — see how much conviction the move carries.

Model: volume multiple = day's volume ÷ normal average volume. Below 1.5x is treated as ordinary, 1.5x-3x as elevated, above 3x as a genuine spike — combined with the size of the price move to rate overall conviction. A real trading decision should weigh more than just these two numbers.
